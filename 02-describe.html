2 · Describe one variable

Counts and percentages for a categorical variable; mean, median, standard deviation and histogram for a continuous one. Lab 2.
Setup: load the ANES data and clean the variables (page 1) 
dataset <- read.csv("data/anes_lab.csv")
dataset$voted_2024 <- ifelse(dataset$V242065 == 4, 1, ifelse(dataset$V242065 %in% c(1, 2, 3), 0, NA))
dataset$like_democrat <- ifelse(dataset$V242432 >= 0 & dataset$V242432 <= 10, dataset$V242432, NA)
dataset$like_trump <- ifelse(dataset$V242435 >= 0 & dataset$V242435 <= 10, dataset$V242435, NA)
dataset$gender <- ifelse(dataset$V241551 %in% c(1, 2, 3, 4), dataset$V241551, NA)

Which statistics for which variable?

Variable type Statistics Graph
Categorical: nominal, ordinal, binary counts, percentages barplot(table(x))
Continuous mean, median, standard deviation, minimum and maximum, quartiles hist(x), boxplot(x)

A categorical variable

Recipe

table(dataset$x, useNA = "ifany")            # counts, including missing
prop.table(table(dataset$x)) * 100           # percentages of the valid answers
barplot(table(dataset$x))

Example from the labs (Lab 2: voted in 2024)

table(dataset$voted_2024, useNA = "ifany")
1round(prop.table(table(dataset$voted_2024)) * 100, 1)
#> 
#>    0    1 <NA> 
#>  835 3933  753 
#> 
#>    0    1 
#> 17.5 82.5
1
round(…, 1) keeps one decimal. table() leaves out NA unless you ask for it, so these are percentages of the respondents who answered.

How to read it

Of the respondents who answered, 82.5% say they voted in 2024 (n = 4768).

A continuous variable

Recipe

mean(dataset$x, na.rm = TRUE)
median(dataset$x, na.rm = TRUE)
sd(dataset$x, na.rm = TRUE)
summary(dataset$x)                 # minimum, quartiles, mean, maximum, number of NAs
hist(dataset$x)

Example from the labs (Lab 2: rating of the Democratic Party, 0–10)

1mean(dataset$like_democrat, na.rm = TRUE)
median(dataset$like_democrat, na.rm = TRUE)
sd(dataset$like_democrat, na.rm = TRUE)
summary(dataset$like_democrat)
#> [1] 4.87644
#> [1] 5
#> [1] 3.405986
#>    Min. 1st Qu.  Median    Mean 3rd Qu.    Max.     NAs 
#>   0.000   2.000   5.000   4.876   8.000  10.000     835
1
na.rm = TRUE removes the missing values for this calculation. Without it, one NA makes the whole result NA, as below. 
mean(dataset$like_democrat)
#> [1] NA
hist(dataset$like_democrat,
     main = "Rating of the Democratic Party",
     xlab = "0 = strongly dislike, 10 = strongly like",
     ylab = "Number of respondents")
TipOne bar per value

By default, the first bar of this histogram counts the 0s and the 1s together (1107 = 832 + 275), so there are 10 bars for 11 values. For a 0–10 scale, set the bar edges yourself:

hist(dataset$like_democrat, breaks = seq(-0.5, 10.5, by = 1))

How to read it

  • Mean and median: compare them. If they are very close, the distribution is approximately symmetric. If the mean is above the median, it is positively (right-) skewed; if below, negatively (left-) skewed. Name the values that pull the mean away from the median.
  • Standard deviation: how far values typically lie from the mean. Judge it against the mean: small compared with the mean means a small spread, large means a large spread.

Mean: 4.88. Median: 5. Standard deviation: 3.41.

Given that the mean and the median are very close, the distribution of the rating of the Democratic Party is approximately symmetric. The standard deviation means that ratings typically lie about 3.4 points from the mean, which is large compared with the mean. The spread of the distribution is thus large: respondents disagree a lot about the party.

Your turn

NoteExercise 2.1 · Lab 2

For the rating of Donald Trump (like_trump, cleaned in the setup above), give the mean, the median and the standard deviation, and plot a histogram. Write one sentence that describes the variable.

TipHint

Same commands as for like_democrat. Remember na.rm = TRUE.

TipSolution 
mean(dataset$like_trump, na.rm = TRUE)
#> [1] 4.035806
median(dataset$like_trump, na.rm = TRUE)
#> [1] 3
sd(dataset$like_trump, na.rm = TRUE)
#> [1] 3.965691
hist(dataset$like_trump, breaks = seq(-0.5, 10.5, by = 1),
     main = "Rating of Donald Trump", xlab = "0 = strongly dislike, 10 = strongly like")

Mean: 4.04. Median: 3. Standard deviation: 3.97.

The mean is above the median, so the distribution is positively (right-) skewed, due to the many high ratings: 29% give 8 to 10, which pulls the mean above the median, while 39% give 0. The standard deviation means that ratings typically lie about 4 points from the mean, which is large compared with the mean. The spread is thus large: the ratings are polarised.
