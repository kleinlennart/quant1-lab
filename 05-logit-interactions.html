5 · Logit, multinomial logit and interactions

Logistic regression with marginal effects, multinomial logistic regression, and interaction terms (moderating effects). Lab 5.
Setup: load the ESS data and clean the variables (as in Labs 4–5) 
dataset <- read.csv("data/ess_lab.csv")
dataset$lrscale <- ifelse(dataset$lrscale %in% c(77, 88, 99), NA, dataset$lrscale)   # left-right, 0-10
dataset$agea <- ifelse(dataset$agea == 999, NA, dataset$agea)
dataset$eduyrs <- ifelse(dataset$eduyrs %in% c(77, 88, 99), NA, dataset$eduyrs)
# Feeling about household income: 1 = living comfortably ... 4 = very difficult
dataset$income_feeling <- ifelse(dataset$hincfel >= 1 & dataset$hincfel <= 4, dataset$hincfel, NA)
dataset$trust_police <- ifelse(dataset$trstplc >= 0 & dataset$trstplc <= 10, dataset$trstplc, NA)

Logistic regression

You need this when the outcome is binary (0/1).

Recipe

model <- glm(y ~ x1 + factor(x2) + x3, data = dataset, family = binomial)
summary(model)                      # read only the signs
library(margins)
summary(margins(model))             # average marginal effects

Example from the labs (Lab 5: who places themselves on the far right, 8–10?)

dataset$far_right <- ifelse(dataset$lrscale >= 8 & dataset$lrscale <= 10, 1,
                            ifelse(dataset$lrscale >= 0 & dataset$lrscale <= 7, 0, NA))
table(dataset$far_right, useNA = "ifany")
#> 
#>     0     1  <NA> 
#> 29289  5151  5074

logit1 <- glm(far_right ~ agea + factor(gndr) + eduyrs + factor(cntry),
              data = dataset, family = binomial)
summary(logit1)$coefficients[1:4, ]    # the first rows; summary(logit1) shows everything
#>                   Estimate   Std. Error    z value      Pr(>|z|)
#> (Intercept)   -2.898430768 0.1245564248 -23.270022 8.921369e-120
#> agea           0.009263302 0.0008841658  10.476883  1.103194e-25
#> factor(gndr)2 -0.403954873 0.0313125011 -12.900754  4.457148e-38
#> eduyrs        -0.013909426 0.0041625897  -3.341532  8.331747e-04
library(margins)
1summary(margins(logit1, variables = c("agea", "gndr", "eduyrs")))
#>  factor     AME     SE        z      p   lower   upper
#>    agea  0.0011 0.0001  10.4921 0.0000  0.0009  0.0013
#>  eduyrs -0.0017 0.0005  -3.3421 0.0008 -0.0027 -0.0007
#>   gndr2 -0.0496 0.0038 -12.8889 0.0000 -0.0571 -0.0421
1
Lab 5 runs summary(margins(logit1)), which also lists every country. variables = keeps the rows you want.

How to read it

  • Coefficients cannot be directly interpreted, except their sign: a positive sign means a higher probability that y = 1, a negative sign a lower one. z value and Pr(>|z|) work like t value and Pr(>|t|) in OLS.
  • Marginal effects (AME) give the size: the increase in percentage points of the predicted probability. Multiply by 100: a marginal effect of -0.13 means that the probability decreases by 13 percentage points.
NoteBeyond the course: odds ratios

The TA’s R summary also shows odds ratios, exp(coef(model)): above 1 means higher odds, below 1 lower odds. The course interprets logits with the sign and the marginal effects, so use those in the exam.

The coefficient for women is negative, so women are less likely than men to place themselves on the far right. The marginal effect is -0.05: net of age, education and the country fixed effects, the probability is 5 percentage points lower for women than for men (statistically significant at the 0.01 level). When age increases by one year, the probability increases by 0.11 percentage points, so about 1.1 points over ten years.

Multinomial logistic regression

You need this when the outcome is categorical with three or more categories.

Recipe

library(nnet)
model <- multinom(y ~ x1 + factor(x2) + x3, data = dataset)
summary(model)
round(coef(model), 3)               # the coefficients alone, easier to read
exp(coef(model))                    # relative risk ratios

Example from the labs (Lab 5: left 0–3, centre 4–6, right 7–10)

dataset$left_right_3cat <- NA
dataset$left_right_3cat[dataset$lrscale >= 0 & dataset$lrscale <= 3] <- 1
dataset$left_right_3cat[dataset$lrscale >= 4 & dataset$lrscale <= 6] <- 2
dataset$left_right_3cat[dataset$lrscale >= 7 & dataset$lrscale <= 10] <- 3

library(nnet)
mlogit1 <- multinom(left_right_3cat ~ agea + factor(gndr) + eduyrs + factor(cntry),
1                    data = dataset, trace = FALSE)
2round(coef(mlogit1)[, 1:4], 3)
#>   (Intercept)   agea factor(gndr)2 eduyrs
#> 2       2.101 -0.003        -0.049 -0.043
#> 3       0.083  0.004        -0.415 -0.035
1
trace = FALSE hides the “iter 10 value …” lines that multinom() prints while it estimates. Lab 5 shows them.
2
summary(mlogit1) is very wide with 26 coefficients and becomes hard to read. This shows the first 4 columns: intercept, age, gender, education.

summary() gives coefficients and standard errors but no p-values. You can compute them yourself:

s <- summary(mlogit1)
z <- s$coefficients / s$standard.errors
round(2 * (1 - pnorm(abs(z)))[, 1:4], 4)            # p-values (two-sided z-test)
#>   (Intercept) agea factor(gndr)2 eduyrs
#> 2      0.0000    0        0.0827      0
#> 3      0.4771    0        0.0000      0

How to read it

  • The reference category is the first one, 1 = left. Row 2 compares centre with left, and row 3 compares right with left.

  • Women:

    • -0.41 in row 3: compared with men, women have lower log-odds of placing themselves on the right rather than the left.
    • -0.05 in row 2: a small difference between centre and left, statistically significant at the 0.10 level only (p = 0.083), not at the 0.05 level.

  • To change the reference category, reorder the levels first. The TA’s R summary does this to make the centre the reference:

    dataset$left_right_3cat <- factor(dataset$left_right_3cat, levels = c(2, 1, 3),
                                  labels = c("Centre", "Left", "Right"))

    The coefficients then compare left and right with the centre, so they differ from the ones above.

Interactions

You need this when the effect of one variable depends on another: “Is the effect of education different for women and men?”

Recipe: x * z in the formula adds x, z and their product x:z.

model <- lm(y ~ x * z + other_controls, data = dataset)

A categorical × a continuous variable

Example from the labs (Lab 5: does the effect of education on left-right differ by gender?)

model_int1 <- lm(lrscale ~ agea + factor(gndr) * eduyrs + factor(cntry), data = dataset)
summary(model_int1)$coefficients[c("agea", "factor(gndr)2", "eduyrs", "factor(gndr)2:eduyrs"), ]
#>                          Estimate   Std. Error   t value     Pr(>|t|)
#> agea                  0.003258086 0.0006722539  4.846511 1.262064e-06
#> factor(gndr)2        -0.181760047 0.0835904597 -2.174411 2.968114e-02
#> eduyrs               -0.021661187 0.0045144608 -4.798178 1.607954e-06
#> factor(gndr)2:eduyrs -0.010748501 0.0059789136 -1.797735 7.222794e-02

How to read it

  • eduyrs = -0.022 is the effect of education for men, the reference group.
  • factor(gndr)2:eduyrs = -0.011 is how much the effect of education differs for women. So for women it is -0.022 + (-0.011) = -0.032 per year.
  • factor(gndr)2 is now the gender gap at 0 years of education, not the average gap.
  • The p-value of the interaction tests whether gender moderates the effect of education, that is, whether the two slopes differ. If the interaction is not statistically significant, we cannot conclude that there is a moderating effect.

When education increases by one year, left-right self-placement decreases by 0.022 points for men and by 0.032 points for women. The interaction is statistically significant at the 0.10 level (p = 0.072) but not at the 0.05 level, so the evidence that gender moderates the effect of education is weak.

Two continuous variables

Example from the labs (Lab 5: does the effect of education depend on how people feel about their income?)

model_int2 <- lm(lrscale ~ agea + factor(gndr) + eduyrs * income_feeling + factor(cntry),
                 data = dataset)
summary(model_int2)$coefficients[c("eduyrs", "income_feeling", "eduyrs:income_feeling"), ]
#>                           Estimate  Std. Error   t value     Pr(>|t|)
#> eduyrs                -0.048424843 0.007528578 -6.432137 1.275072e-10
#> income_feeling        -0.250799405 0.049275684 -5.089719 3.605198e-07
#> eduyrs:income_feeling  0.007794048 0.003602709  2.163386 3.051854e-02

The effect of education at each value of income_feeling is eduyrs + eduyrs:income_feeling × value:

k <- 1:4
data.frame(income_feeling = k,
           effect_of_education = coef(model_int2)[["eduyrs"]] + k * coef(model_int2)[["eduyrs:income_feeling"]])
#>   income_feeling effect_of_education
#> 1              1         -0.04063080
#> 2              2         -0.03283675
#> 3              3         -0.02504270
#> 4              4         -0.01724865
Warningincome_feeling runs “backwards”

1 = living comfortably, 4 = very difficult. A higher value means harder to get by.

The interaction is 0.0078, statistically significant at the 0.05 level: income feeling moderates the effect of education. When income feeling increases by one unit (harder to get by), the effect of education changes by 0.0078. Among those living comfortably, each year of education moves the score 0.041 points to the left; among those who find it very difficult, only 0.017 points.

NoteExercise 5.1 · Lab 5

Using trust in the police (trust_police, cleaned in the setup):

  1. Create high_trust_police: 0 = 5 or less, 1 = 6 or more.
  2. Estimate a logistic regression of high_trust_police on age, gender, education and country fixed effects.
  3. Calculate the marginal effects and interpret the one for gender.
TipSolution 
dataset$high_trust_police <- ifelse(dataset$trust_police >= 6, 1,
                                    ifelse(dataset$trust_police <= 5, 0, NA))
logit_trust <- glm(high_trust_police ~ agea + factor(gndr) + eduyrs + factor(cntry),
                   data = dataset, family = binomial)
summary(logit_trust)$coefficients[1:4, ]
#>                  Estimate   Std. Error   z value     Pr(>|z|)
#> (Intercept)   0.850615908 0.0804013635 10.579620 3.704559e-26
#> agea          0.003621234 0.0006432647  5.629462 1.807725e-08
#> factor(gndr)2 0.089600180 0.0233758692  3.833020 1.265795e-04
#> eduyrs        0.028043358 0.0031665365  8.856161 8.281692e-19
summary(margins(logit_trust, variables = c("agea", "gndr", "eduyrs")))
#>  factor    AME     SE      z      p  lower  upper
#>    agea 0.0007 0.0001 5.6371 0.0000 0.0005 0.0009
#>  eduyrs 0.0054 0.0006 8.8853 0.0000 0.0042 0.0066
#>   gndr2 0.0173 0.0045 3.8303 0.0001 0.0084 0.0261

The marginal effect for women is 0.017: net of the other variables and the country fixed effects, the probability of high trust in the police is 1.7 percentage points higher for women than for men (statistically significant at the 0.01 level).

NoteExercise 5.2 · Lab 5

Estimate an OLS regression of trust_police with an interaction between gender and education (plus age and country fixed effects). What is the effect of education for men and for women?

TipSolution 
model_t1 <- lm(trust_police ~ agea + factor(gndr) * eduyrs + factor(cntry), data = dataset)
summary(model_t1)$coefficients[c("factor(gndr)2", "eduyrs", "factor(gndr)2:eduyrs"), ]
#>                         Estimate  Std. Error   t value     Pr(>|t|)
#> factor(gndr)2         0.36865848 0.080636169  4.571875 4.848818e-06
#> eduyrs                0.02621906 0.004439593  5.905736 3.540173e-09
#> factor(gndr)2:eduyrs -0.02120391 0.005838034 -3.632029 2.815666e-04

For men, each year of education raises trust in the police by 0.026 points. For women, by 0.026 + (-0.021) = 0.005. The interaction is statistically significant at the 0.01 level, so gender moderates the effect of education: education matters much less for women’s trust in the police.

NoteExercise 5.3 · Lab 5

Estimate an OLS regression of trust_police with an interaction between income feeling and education (plus age, gender and country fixed effects). How does the effect of education change with income feeling?

TipSolution
