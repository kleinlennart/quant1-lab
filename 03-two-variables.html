3 · Two variables

Crosstab and chi-square test, group means and t-test, correlation. Labs 2–3.
Setup: load the ANES data and clean the variables (page 1) 
dataset <- read.csv("data/anes_lab.csv")
dataset$voted_2024 <- ifelse(dataset$V242065 == 4, 1, ifelse(dataset$V242065 %in% c(1, 2, 3), 0, NA))
dataset$like_democrat <- ifelse(dataset$V242432 >= 0 & dataset$V242432 <= 10, dataset$V242432, NA)
dataset$like_trump <- ifelse(dataset$V242435 >= 0 & dataset$V242435 <= 10, dataset$V242435, NA)
dataset$gender <- ifelse(dataset$V241551 %in% c(1, 2, 3, 4), dataset$V241551, NA)
dataset$age <- ifelse(dataset$V241458x >= 18 & dataset$V241458x <= 80, dataset$V241458x, NA)

# Satisfaction with democracy: 1 = very, 2 = fairly, 4 = not very, 5 = not at all satisfied
dataset$swd <- ifelse(dataset$V242439 %in% c(1, 2, 4, 5), dataset$V242439, NA)
dataset$swd_binary <- ifelse(dataset$swd %in% c(1, 2), 1, ifelse(dataset$swd %in% c(4, 5), 0, NA))

dataset$age_category <- NA
dataset$age_category[dataset$age <= 30] <- 0
dataset$age_category[dataset$age >= 31 & dataset$age <= 60] <- 1
dataset$age_category[dataset$age >= 61] <- 2

Choose the test

Variables Describe with Test
Categorical × categorical crosstab with percentages chi-square test: chisq.test()
Continuous by a binary group the mean in each group, boxplot t-test: t.test()
Continuous × continuous scatter plot correlation: cor(), cor.test()

All three tests start from the same null hypothesis (H0): there is no relationship between the two variables in the population. The p-value is the probability of finding an association at least as strong as the one in the sample if H0 were true. If it is below 0.10, 0.05 or 0.01, the result is statistically significant at that level and you reject H0. The course reports these three levels.

The p-value is what you mainly read for these tests. It does not tell you how strong the relationship is. The chi-square test doesn’t give a direction either, so look at the percentages for that.

Two categorical variables

Recipe

tab <- table(dataset$x, dataset$y)    # rows = x, columns = y
tab
prop.table(tab, margin = 1) * 100     # row percentages: each row adds up to 100
prop.table(tab, margin = 2) * 100     # column percentages: each column adds up to 100
chisq.test(tab)

Example from the labs (Lab 3: are voters more often satisfied with democracy?)

1tab <- table(dataset$swd_binary, dataset$voted_2024)
tab
2round(prop.table(tab, margin = 2) * 100, 1)
3chisq.test(tab)
#>    
#>        0    1
#>   0  365 1147
#>   1  415 2561
#>    
#>        0    1
#>   0 46.8 30.9
#>   1 53.2 69.1
#> 
#>  Pearson's Chi-squared test with Yates' continuity correction
#> 
#> data:  tab
#> X-squared = 71.87, df = 1, p-value < 2.2e-16
1
Rows: satisfied with democracy (1) or not (0). Columns: voted (1) or not (0).
2
Column percentages, because we compare the columns, non-voters with voters: the share of each group that is satisfied.
3
The output gives X-squared, the chi-square statistic; df, which is (rows − 1) × (columns − 1); and the p-value.

How to read it

The chi-square test gives X² = 71.9 (df = 1, p < 0.001). The association is statistically significant at the 0.01 level, so we reject the null hypothesis of no association between voting and satisfaction with democracy. The test doesn’t show the direction, but the percentages do: 69% of voters are satisfied, compared with 53% of non-voters.

NoteNotes on the output
  • On a 2 × 2 table, R applies Yates’ continuity correction by default, and the output says so (“with Yates’ continuity correction”). On larger tables it doesn’t.
  • A warning “Chi-squared approximation may be incorrect” means some expected cell counts are small. Look at them with chisq.test(tab)$expected.

A continuous variable by group

Recipe

aggregate(y ~ group, data = dataset, FUN = mean)   # the mean of y in each group
t.test(y ~ group, data = dataset)                  # group must have exactly two values
boxplot(y ~ group, data = dataset)

Example from the labs (Lab 2: mean rating of the Democratic Party by vote)

aggregate(like_democrat ~ voted_2024, data = dataset, FUN = mean)
#>   voted_2024 like_democrat
#> 1          0      4.373077
#> 2          1      4.949704

Non-voters: 4.37. Voters: 4.95. There is therefore a difference in the means, suggesting an association between voting and the rating of the Democratic Party in this sample. Whether it holds in the population is what the t-test below checks.

Example from the labs (Lab 3: is satisfaction with democracy different for voters and non-voters?)

t.test(swd ~ voted_2024, data = dataset)
#> 
#>  Welch Two Sample t-test
#> 
#> data:  swd by voted_2024
#> t = 8.2327, df = 1056.3, p-value = 5.355e-16
#> alternative hypothesis: true difference in means between group 0 and group 1 is not equal to 0
#> 95 percent confidence interval:
#>  0.3044511 0.4949929
#> sample estimates:
#> mean in group 0 mean in group 1 
#>        2.973077        2.573355
boxplot(swd ~ voted_2024, data = dataset,
        names = c("Didn't vote", "Voted"), ylab = "1 = very satisfied ... 5 = not at all")

How to read it

  • t is the test statistic, and df is the degrees of freedom. R runs Welch’s t-test by default, so df can have decimals.
  • p-value: the test of H0 that the two means are equal.
  • 95 percent confidence interval: for the difference between the means, group 0 minus group 1.
  • sample estimates: the two group means, which give you the direction.

Non-voters have a mean satisfaction score of 2.97 and voters 2.57 (t = 8.23, p < 0.001). The difference is statistically significant at the 0.01 level, so we reject the null hypothesis that the means are not different. A lower score means more satisfied, so voters are more satisfied with democracy than non-voters.

WarningTwo traps in this example
  • The scale runs backwards: 1 = very satisfied, 5 = not at all. A lower mean means more satisfied.
  • There is no code 3, so the t-test treats the step from “fairly” (2) to “not very” (4) as two points. The labs use the t-test this way anyway.
WarningError: “grouping factor must have exactly 2 levels”

t.test() needs a group variable with exactly two values. Check it with table(dataset$group).

  • One value: the recode went wrong. In the Lab 3 script, the line that creates voted_2024 uses V242066. Change it to V242065, as in Lab 2.
  • More than two values (e.g. gender with four categories): use a chi-square test, or keep only two groups.

Two continuous variables

Recipe

cor(dataset$x, dataset$y, use = "complete.obs")   # use only rows where both are not NA
cor.test(dataset$x, dataset$y)                    # adds a p-value
plot(dataset$x, dataset$y)

Example from the labs (Lab 2: rating of the Democratic Party and age)

cor(dataset$like_democrat, dataset$age, use = "complete.obs")
#> [1] 0.02571812
cor.test(dataset$like_democrat, dataset$age)
#> 
#>  Pearson's product-moment correlation
#> 
#> data:  dataset$like_democrat and dataset$age
#> t = 1.7233, df = 4487, p-value = 0.0849
#> alternative hypothesis: true correlation is not equal to 0
#> 95 percent confidence interval:
#>  -0.003539169  0.054931412
#> sample estimates:
#>        cor 
#> 0.02571812
plot(dataset$age, dataset$like_democrat, xlab = "Age", ylab = "Rating of the Democratic Party")

How to read it

  • The correlation r runs from −1 to +1. The sign gives the direction (positive or negative), and the course’s labels give the strength of the linear association: |r| below 0.10 negligible, from 0.10 weak, from 0.30 moderate, from 0.50 strong, from 0.75 very strong.
  • Then say what it means in a plain sentence, as in the course’s model answer: “a positive, moderate association between Grade and Left-right, so that the more right-wing someone is, the higher their grade”.

Correlation: 0.03. There is a positive, negligible association between age and the rating of the Democratic Party: older respondents rate the party only very slightly higher. It is statistically significant at the 0.10 level (p = 0.085), but a statistically significant association is not always a strong one.

The ratings take only 11 values, so many points sit on top of each other. plot(jitter(dataset$age), jitter(dataset$like_democrat)) spreads them out a little.

Your turn

NoteExercise 3.1 · Lab 2

What is the correlation between the rating of Donald Trump (like_trump) and age?

TipSolution 
cor(dataset$like_trump, dataset$age, use = "complete.obs")
#> [1] 0.03059228

Correlation: 0.03. There is a positive, negligible association between age and the rating of Trump: age makes almost no difference to how much respondents like him.

NoteExercise 3.2 · Lab 3

Is satisfaction with democracy (swd_binary) associated with gender (gender)? Give the percentages and the test.

TipHint

Both variables are categorical. To compare the genders, take percentages within each gender.

TipSolution 
tab_g <- table(dataset$swd_binary, dataset$gender)
round(prop.table(tab_g, margin = 2) * 100, 1)   # within each gender (columns)
#>    
#>        1    2    3    4
#>   0 30.2 35.1 69.8 55.6
#>   1 69.8 64.9 30.2 44.4
chisq.test(tab_g)
#> 
#>  Pearson's Chi-squared test
#> 
#> data:  tab_g
#> X-squared = 42.888, df = 3, p-value = 2.6e-09

X² = 42.9 (df = 3, p < 0.001). The association is statistically significant at the 0.01 level, so we reject the null hypothesis of no association between gender and satisfaction with democracy. 70% of men and 65% of women are satisfied.

NoteExercise 3.3 · Lab 3

Is satisfaction with democracy (swd_binary) associated with age, treating age as continuous?

TipHint

Age is continuous and swd_binary is a binary group: compare the mean age of the two groups.

TipSolution 
t.test(age ~ swd_binary, data = dataset)
#> 
#>  Welch Two Sample t-test
#> 
#> data:  age by swd_binary
#> t = -13.517, df = 3011.1, p-value < 2.2e-16
#> alternative hypothesis: true difference in means between group 0 and group 1 is not equal to 0
#> 95 percent confidence interval:
#>  -8.211521 -6.131063
#> sample estimates:
#> mean in group 0 mean in group 1 
#>        48.71067        55.88196
boxplot(age ~ swd_binary, data = dataset, names = c("Dissatisfied", "Satisfied"), ylab = "Age")

The dissatisfied are 48.71 years old on average, and the satisfied 55.88 (p < 0.001). The difference is statistically significant at the 0.01 level, so we reject the null hypothesis that the means are not different: people who are satisfied with democracy are older on average.

NoteExercise 3.4 · Lab 3

Is satisfaction with democracy (swd_binary) associated with the age categories (age_category: 0 = 30 or younger, 1 = 31–60, 2 = 61 or older)?

TipSolution 
tab_a <- table(dataset$age_category, dataset$swd_binary)
round(prop.table(tab_a, margin = 1) * 100, 1)   # within each age group (rows)
#>    
#>        0    1
#>   0 47.3 52.7
#>   1 37.6 62.4
#>   2 24.7 75.3
chisq.test(tab_a)
#> 
#>  Pearson's Chi-squared test
#> 
#> data:  tab_a
#> X-squared = 121.52, df = 2, p-value < 2.2e-16

X² = 121.5 (df = 2, p < 0.001). The association is statistically significant at the 0.01 level, so we reject the null hypothesis of no association. Dissatisfaction falls with age: 47% of those aged 30 or younger are dissatisfied, against 25% of those aged 61 or older.
